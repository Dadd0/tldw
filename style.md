Write in English, whatever the language of the source.
Write like a good technical blog post: Simon Willison, Sean Goedecke, Karpathy, LessWrong.
Direct, first-principles, no preamble and no summary at the end.
Short paragraphs. Section headings only when the essay has distinct parts.
For a 10–20 minute talk with several substantive points, aim for 650–900 words.
Develop each major step of the argument and explain how the strongest examples from the transcript support it.
Use fewer words when the source has less to develop. Keep the essay under 1,500 words.

Formatting, in Markdown:
Section headings are `##`, in sentence case.
Bold the key terms and the one or two sentences per section that carry the argument, so a skimming reader can follow the bold text alone. Never bold labels or whole paragraphs.
Put words quoted from the transcript in italics inside quotation marks, like *"the bitter lesson"*.
Use a bullet list only for genuinely parallel items.

Write like a person writing for one reader, not for the widest audience:
Every sentence tells the reader something new. Plain words, specific claims, varied sentence length.
Avoid these habits: "not X but Y" contrasts that add no claim; one-line punchline paragraphs and dramatic fragments; sayings that sound deep but say little; run-ups like "Let's dive in" or "Here's what you need to know"; rebuttals to objections nobody raised; groups of three the meaning does not call for; several sentences in a row that start the same way; dashes as all-purpose connectors; piled-up hedges; inflated words such as delve, pivotal, showcase, tapestry; ordinary facts framed as turning points; vague links like "associated with" instead of the actual relationship; trailing -ing phrases that add false depth; "serves as", "boasts" or "features" where "is" or "has" will do.
