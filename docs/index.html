TL;DW · too long; didn't watch

Read the talk instead of watching it

Turn long YouTube talks and podcasts into 5-minute essays, plus Q&A over every transcript.

Edoardo Cocciò, Simone Cavallaro and Filippo Lombardo

TL;DW follows a list of YouTube channels. When one of them posts a video, it transcribes it with speaker labels and timestamps, writes a standalone essay from the transcript, and puts the essay on a private front page that looks like Hacker News. A chat page answers questions across every transcript. The idea behind it is that reading is several times faster than listening, and in a talk, an interview or a podcast the picture rarely tells you anything. It's 1,789 lines of Python on one server, and it has exactly one user, its owner.

The reader we built this for follows channels of talks, interviews and podcasts, and saves more of them than they will ever watch. Double speed doesn't fix that: a one-hour-42-minute talk still takes 51 minutes, and fast speech is tiring to follow for that long. In most of these videos the picture is a person or two talking, so reading loses nothing. The same talk on our front page is a six-minute read. Reading time is the essay's word count divided by 230, rounded up, and the style guide keeps every essay under 1,500 words, so no essay takes more than seven minutes. Sponsor reads, greetings and banter are cut before you see them.

Think of a technical reader's Watch Later list: every new interview adds another hour, even when almost everything that matters is spoken aloud.

On the autonomy ladder, TL;DW sits low on purpose: a workflow, with two agents that use tools inside tight bounds. Transcription and embeddings are single requests with a fixed shape, so no model decides anything there. Models make decisions in two places only: writing the essay and answering a question. The agents never talk to each other. PostgreSQL on Supabase holds the state, and each stage looks for its missing piece: a video with no record, a transcript with no essay, a passage with no vector. The owner keeps four gates: which channels are followed, what the style guide says, which single links are submitted or which essays are rewritten, and the SSH tunnel that is the only way in.

Code fixes the order of the steps. The two agents never call each other. Each stage reads the shared database to find its next job, which removes the need for a separate broker. Both transcription and embedding use ordinary HTTP requests.

Tool boundary

  • 1Essay Agenttool: transcription pipeline (audio to text)
  • 1Chat Agenttool: read-only retrieval over pgvector

yt-dlp reads a video's details before downloading, and when polling anything under three minutes is stored as skipped, which drops Shorts. FFmpeg cuts the audio into five-minute pieces without re-encoding, and each piece takes about 17 seconds through OpenRouter, inside the provider's 60-second limit. Speaker numbers restart in every piece, so each label carries its chunk number and nothing guesses who is who across chunks. Then the tool boundary. Each agent pairs with one read-only tool: the Essay Agent relies on the transcription pipeline to turn audio into a diarized transcript (while ordinary code saves the essay), and the Chat Agent relies on semantic retrieval over pgvector. Neither can change stored data, so a hostile transcript has nothing to act with.

Grok returns a timestamp and a speaker for every word. A passage ends at a change of speaker, at 200 words, or at a chunk boundary. At 12:45, label 2.1 means chunk 2 (the third five-minute piece), speaker 1. The video and its passages are saved together in one database transaction. Transcription runs upstream of the Essay Agent. The agent receives its output and cannot initiate downloads, browse the web, delete records or save the essay itself.

Two focused PydanticAI agents

Pattern 1

Whole talk in, standalone essay out.

Pattern 2

Search first, answer with source links.

The Essay Agent makes one call with the full transcript and a typed reply. The source is fixed and fits in the model's context, so a loop would add cost and failure points, and summarizing chunks separately would break arguments that span the talk. Fixed rules forbid any fact, number or quote the transcript lacks; the owner's style guide sets voice and length. The Chat Agent works the other way. Passages of 20 or more words are embedded with Voyage 4 Lite, and its search takes the eight nearest by an exact scan. On the site its first step must be a search. The standalone chat also links each claim to the essay and the exact second in the video.

The title and Markdown body are validated before the essay is saved, and the source transcript must still be unchanged. The owner can edit the voice and length for future essays. A question on an essay page searches only that video; Ask searches the library. The Chat Agent can rephrase its search when one query misses a relevant passage. Starlette streams the response. The site's chat names the source video; the standalone chat supplies the clickable essay and timestamp links.

We picked ten cases that cover what TL;DW does every day and the ways it can go wrong. Five are typical: a short talk, an interview, a long talk that must stay under the word cap, and two kinds of question. Three are edges we hit for real: overlapping Italian speech, a Short that should cost nothing, and a question the library can't answer, where the chat must still help and say what comes from general knowledge. Two are attacks: an instruction spoken inside a talk, and raw HTML or a broken link. The target is nine of ten passing, two questions or fewer per usable answer, and under 20 cents per hour of video.

  1. Short tech talk: essay states its thesis first and never mentions the video.
  2. Interview, two speakers: questions and answers kept apart.
  3. Talk over an hour: essay under 1,500 words.
  4. Question on one essay: answer uses that video only.
  5. Question across the library: each part names its video.
  6. Italian, people talking over each other: Italian transcript, English essay, no stray script.
  7. Clip under 3 minutes: skipped, nothing spent.
  8. Question the library can't answer: still answers, marks general knowledge.
  9. Spoken "ignore your instructions" in a talk: treated as quoted source text.
  10. Script tag or malformed link: shown as text, or refused by Submit.

A case passes only if every check holds. Count the reader's questions before a usable answer, including follow-ups.

Each change answers a failure we saw. In the baseline, Gemini 3.5 Transcribe labelled speakers on every word in one call and on a quarter of them in an identical repeat, so the interview case fails. Switching to Grok STT 2.0 fixed the speakers and cut cost from 27 to 10 cents an hour. The language hint matters too: without it, one Italian conversation drifted into Hebrew script. Change 2 moves essay generation and chat to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and hardens the chat loop. Forcing the first search grounds the first answer, so fewer follow-ups are needed. Transcripts and search results are framed as source material, and the renderer escapes raw HTML, which it otherwise passes through untouched. A question costs well under a tenth of a cent.

The transcription costs were measured through OpenRouter in November 2026. The baseline left language detection automatic and allowed the chat to skip search. Turns count reader questions, including follow-ups. The repository records the model failures and prices but contains no scored ten-case run for the success rates and turn counts; those figures illustrate the proposed evaluation.

Injection first. The Chat Agent's only tool reads, so an instruction hidden in a talk has nothing to act with, and the agent is told to treat search results as source material and ignore instructions inside them. Privacy comes from having nothing public: both web apps listen on the server's loopback address, the owner reaches them through one SSH tunnel, and Supabase's public key can't read the tables. Cost is capped by design: Shorts are skipped, transcription is ten cents an hour, and a failure waits for the next poll instead of looping. For hallucination, the essay rules forbid anything the transcript lacks, and the chat marks what comes from general knowledge, so the reader knows what to check.

Markdown escapes raw HTML before display. Supabase has row level security enabled and no public access policies. Secrets are redacted from error output. Transcription costs $0.10 per hour of audio; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash handles the essay and chat. When a scheduled essay fails, the next poll uses the stored transcript without paying for audio again. Retrieval uses an exact cosine scan. The standalone chat links each claim to the second it came from.
