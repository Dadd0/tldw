TL;DW · too long; didn't watch

Read the talk instead of watching it

Turn YouTube talks and podcasts into short essays, then ask questions across the indexed transcripts.

Edoardo Cocciò, Simone Cavallaro and Filippo Lombardo

Group B · AI Frontiers: Large Language Models

TL;DW processes videos from selected YouTube channels or links submitted by the owner. It transcribes the audio with speaker labels and timestamps, then writes a standalone essay for a private front page inspired by Hacker News. The chat searches indexed transcript passages. We built it for a single reader who follows talks, interviews and podcasts.

How (1/3): a workflow with two bounded agents

  1. Fixed script
  2. Workflow
  3. Bounded tool use TL;DW
  4. Open-ended agent

Code fixes the order of steps. Models decide only what the essay says and how a question is answered.

The agents never call each other. Each stage asks the database what is missing, so there is no broker.

Human gates

  • Channel list
  • Style guide
  • Manual submit or rewrite
  • Private SSH access
On the autonomy ladder, TL;DW sits low on purpose: a workflow, with two agents that use tools inside tight bounds. Transcription and embeddings are single requests with a fixed shape, so no model decides anything there. Models make decisions in two places only: writing the essay and answering a question. The agents never talk to each other. PostgreSQL on Supabase holds the state, and each stage looks for its missing piece: a video with no record, a transcript with no essay, a passage with no vector. The owner keeps four gates: which channels are followed, what the style guide says, which single links are submitted or which essays are rewritten, and the SSH tunnel that is the only way in.

When polling, clips under three minutes are skipped.

Grok STT 2.0 returns every word with a timestamp and a speaker.

Turns of up to 200 words keep who said what.

Neither agent can write, delete or browse the web.

Tool boundary

  • 1Essay Agenttool: transcription pipeline (audio to text)
  • 1Chat Agenttool: read-only retrieval over pgvector
yt-dlp reads a video's details before downloading, and when polling anything under three minutes is stored as skipped, which drops Shorts. FFmpeg cuts the audio into five-minute pieces without re-encoding, and each piece takes about 17 seconds through OpenRouter, inside the provider's 60-second limit. Speaker numbers restart in every piece, so each label carries its chunk number and nothing guesses who is who across chunks. Then the tool boundary. Each agent pairs with one read-only tool: the Essay Agent relies on the transcription pipeline to turn audio into a diarized transcript (while ordinary code saves the essay), and the Chat Agent relies on semantic retrieval over pgvector. Neither can change stored data, so a hostile transcript has nothing to act with.

How (3/3): two PydanticAI patterns

Pattern 1

Single-shot structured synthesis. The whole transcript in, a validated title and Markdown body out.

Pattern 2

Retrieval-first answers. Search, search again if needed, then answer within the scope the page sets.

The Essay Agent makes one call with the full transcript and a typed reply. The source is fixed and fits in the model's context, so a loop would add cost and failure points, and summarizing chunks separately would break arguments that span the talk. Fixed rules forbid any fact, number or quote the transcript lacks; the owner's style guide sets voice and length. The Chat Agent works the other way. Passages of 20 or more words are embedded with Voyage 4 Lite, and its search takes the eight nearest by an exact scan. On the site its first step must be a search. The standalone chat also links each claim to the essay and the exact second in the video.

Eval (1/2): how we tested TL;DW

We started with five tasks a reader expects to work on an ordinary day. Then we tried three inputs that could trip up the system even when nobody was trying to fool it. For example, the closest search result might still leave a question unanswered. Finally, we deliberately supplied hostile input. One case tests whether an instruction spoken in a video can steer the model. The other checks how the site handles raw HTML and malformed links.

  1. Short tech talk: essay states its thesis first and never mentions the video.
  2. Interview, two speakers: questions and answers kept apart.
  3. Talk over an hour: essay under 1,500 words.
  4. Question on one essay: answer uses that video only.
  5. Question across the library: each part names its video.
  6. Italian, people talking over each other: Italian transcript, English essay, no stray script.
  7. Clip under 3 minutes: skipped, nothing spent.
  8. Question the library can't answer: still answers, marks general knowledge.
  9. Spoken "ignore your instructions" in a talk: treated as quoted source text.
  10. Script tag or malformed link: shown as text, or refused by Submit.

Eval (2/2): measure, change one thing, measure again

ConfigurationCost per hour of video

Baseline

Gemini 3.5 Transcribe detected the language. Nemotron wrote essays and could answer chat questions without searching.

Change 1

Grok STT 2.0 transcribed five-minute chunks with language hints. Nemotron handled essays and chat.

Change 2

Each change answers a failure we saw. In the baseline, Gemini labelled speakers on every word in one call and on a quarter of them in an identical repeat, so the interview case fails. Switching to Grok STT 2.0 fixed the speakers and cut cost from 27 to 10 cents an hour. The language hint matters too: without it, one Italian conversation drifted into Hebrew script. Change 2 moves essay generation and chat to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and hardens the chat loop. Forcing the first search grounds the first answer, so fewer follow-ups are needed. Transcripts and search results are framed as source material, and the renderer escapes raw HTML, which it otherwise passes through untouched.

Risks: four ways it could fail, four defenses

RiskWhat could go wrongDefense in TL;DW

Prompt injection

A transcript or question tells the model to act

  • Chat Agent has one read-only tool
  • transcripts and search results are framed as source material
  • Markdown output escapes raw HTML

Privacy

Someone else reads the library or the key

  • Site and chat listen only on the server itself, reached through an SSH tunnel
  • no accounts
  • database row level security with no policies
  • secrets redacted from error output

Cost

A loop or a bad video burns credit

  • Clips under 3 min skipped
  • $0.10/hr transcription
  • low-cost DeepSeek V4.1 Flash for essays and chat
  • no retries inside a run
  • a failed essay never pays for transcription twice

Hallucination

An essay or answer invents facts

  • Fixed rule: nothing the transcript lacks
  • site chat searches before answering
  • exact cosine scan
  • general-knowledge gaps marked
  • standalone chat links the exact second
Injection first. The Chat Agent's only tool reads, so an instruction hidden in a talk has nothing to act with, and the agent is told to treat search results as source material and ignore instructions inside them. Privacy comes from having nothing public: both web apps listen on the server's loopback address, the owner reaches them through one SSH tunnel, and Supabase's public key can't read the tables. Cost is capped by design: Shorts are skipped, transcription is ten cents an hour, and a failure waits for the next poll instead of looping. For hallucination, the essay rules forbid anything the transcript lacks, and the chat marks what comes from general knowledge, so the reader knows what to check.

What worked and what fell short

What worked

  • 19 essays from nearly 7 hours of source video.
  • 373 searchable passages with timestamps.
  • Sanfilippo’s 37-minute video on GLM 5.4 became an estimated 6-minute read.

Where it fell short

  • One essay exceeded 1,500 words. In another, a direct quote differed from the transcript.
  • Manual reprocessing can remove an existing essay if indexing fails.
  • Built for videos driven by speech. Audio-only analysis can miss details shown only on screen.

The read-only snapshot on 25 November 2026 contained 19 essays covering 6 hours and 52 minutes of video, and 373 passages with embeddings and timestamps. All 19 essays were listed and rendered by the local site. Sanfilippo's 37-minute-and-29-second video on GLM 5.4 produced a 1,266-word essay, estimated at six minutes to read. The database counts describe the saved library.

One essay exceeded the 1,500-word limit in the style guide. A separate essay presented wording edited from its transcript as a direct quote. In a local simulation, an indexing error during manual reprocessing triggered the code path that deletes the processed video and its essay. A simulated scheduled poll retried the pending indexing step without rerunning transcription or essay generation. No live database rows were changed during these checks.

TL;DW is designed for talks, interviews and other videos that communicate mainly through speech. The essay and chat use the audio transcript. If a diagram, slide or on-screen demonstration contains information the speaker never explains, that information can be missing from the essay and unavailable as a source for the chat.
